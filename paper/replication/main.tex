\begin{abstract}
In 2004 I submitted a dissertation that proposed a two-stage model of visual
attention for active vision: a bottom-up stage of region-based features and a
neural field, and a symbolic second stage that tracks a few \emph{object files}
and inhibits return to \emph{objects} rather than to locations. Its central
claim---that object-based inhibition of return is what a serial attention system
needs in a dynamic scene---rested on one simulation experiment without
intervals and on demonstrations. Twenty years later I reimplemented the model and tested it again: 24 of the thesis's own
experimental findings (among them its one quantitative test of the central claim), that claim under a protocol with predictions
committed before each confirmatory run, and its scanpaths against human gaze.
Twenty-two of its twenty-five experimental findings replicate and three
replicate in part. The central claim holds:
on 30 fresh scenes per regime, object-based inhibition reaches new objects sooner
and keeps cycling through a scene more evenly than space-based inhibition, also
against a motion-compensated spatial baseline and under two parameter sets.
On natural video (DAVIS~2017) the test is inconclusive, because the bottom-up first stage attends mostly to things the instance masks do not name.
On still images the model's scanpaths are only slightly better than chance and
do not beat a constant central path, which the thesis never claimed they would.
The more general result is about replication itself. A first, approximate
reimplementation \emph{refuted} the central claim, robustly and for reasons that
looked principled; the refutation was an artefact of two infidelities---a
symmetry feature that normalised where the original thresholded, and neural
field parameters taken from a function's defaults rather than from the deployed
system. Both were found only by re-running the thesis's own small experiments,
not by the downstream benchmark that they corrupted.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A vision system that can afford to process only a small part of what it sees
has to decide where to look next, and then not look there again. Models of
visual attention in the tradition of \citet{koch1985} and \citet{itti1998} make
that decision on a map: features are computed in parallel, fused into a saliency
map, the maximum is selected, and the selected \emph{location} is suppressed so
that the next maximum is somewhere else---inhibition of return. My 2004
dissertation \citep{backer2004} argued that this breaks as soon as the scene
moves. A location tag inhibits a place, but the object that was examined there
has moved on; information extracted at a fixation is bound to coordinates that
no longer hold it. The model it proposed therefore split selection in two: a
first stage that keeps a handful of salient regions alive over time in a dynamic
neural field, and a second, symbolic stage that maintains an \emph{object file}
\citep{kahneman1992} per region and attaches inhibition of return to the object
rather than to the place. Its central claim is that in multi-object dynamic
scenes this is the better allocation of a serial process. Parts of the model
were published in English \citep{backer2001,backer2003}; the thesis itself is in
German.

Twenty years later I reimplemented it. The reason to re-test rather than only
re-read is that the central claim was never tested the way such a claim would be
tested now. Its quantitative support in the thesis is a single experiment
(\S9.2, reproduced here as Section~\ref{sec:worldmodel}): 50 runs per condition
on simulated saliency maps, reported as means without intervals, against one
hand-configured baseline. Everything else is demonstration---figures of a system
behaving sensibly on scenes chosen by its author. That was normal for the field
and the time; it is not enough to know whether the claim is true. The mechanism
has also acquired a second life: a small symbolic model of what is in a scene,
deciding where an expensive process should look next, is the shape of problem a
vision-language model with a token budget now faces. That use is the subject of
separate work and is not evaluated here, but it is what made the question worth
the effort.

\paragraph{What this paper is, and is not.} It is a reimplementation and a
re-test by the original author, with access to the thesis text and to part of the
original source code. It is not an independent replication, and the difference
matters in both directions: I could resolve ambiguities that an outsider could
not, and I had every incentive to find that my own thesis was right. The
protocol in Section~\ref{sec:protocol} is built around the second point. The
original sources cannot be released (I do not hold the rights); what was taken
from them is reported as facts---parameter values, algorithmic steps---in
Section~\ref{sec:sources} and Appendix~\ref{app:params}.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A replication dossier: 25 experimental findings of the thesis, each
        rebuilt, measured and given a verdict (22 replicated, 3 partially), all
        regenerated by one command (Section~\ref{sec:dossier}).
  \item A confirmatory test of the thesis's central claim, object-based versus
        space-based inhibition of return in dynamic scenes, with a strengthened
        baseline, fresh scenes and predictions committed before the run
        (Section~\ref{sec:h1}), and a stated-inconclusive test on natural video.
  \item An evaluation of the model's scanpaths against human gaze on MIT1003,
        with two methodological findings about scoring scanpaths
        (Section~\ref{sec:h4}).
  \item A case study in how a reimplementation goes wrong: a negative result
        that survived three rounds of honest analysis and was about the
        reimplementation, not the model (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{The 2004 model}
\label{sec:model}

The model has three processing stages and two selection steps between them
(Figure~\ref{fig:architecture}). Preattentive computations run over the whole
image; the first selection step reduces them to a few candidate regions;
\emph{semiattentive} computations maintain symbolic descriptions of those few
over time; the second selection step picks one of them for the attentive stage,
where expensive operations such as recognition run on that region alone. The
middle stage is what distinguishes the model: conventional architectures step
from a saliency map straight to one focus, so anything they extract is bound to
a location, and anything they track has to be tracked by that location.

\subsection{Stage one: region-based features and a neural field}
The features are chosen for one purpose: to respond to \emph{objects} rather
than to pixels, so that what the second stage tracks is object-like from the
start. Each is computed on regions obtained by a fast segmentation, and each
returns an absolute value in $[0,1]$ rather than a map normalised to its own
maximum---a point that matters later (Section~\ref{sec:findingA}).
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Colour contrast.} Transform to a perceptually uniform space
        (the mathematical transform to Munsell, \citealp{miyahara1988}); region
        growing with a variance-dependent threshold; contrast of each region to
        its neighbours weighted by shared border; a sigmoid.
  \item \textbf{Eccentricity.} Edge-bounded regions; second-order central
        moments; $\varepsilon = \frac{(m_{20}-m_{02})^2 + 4m_{11}^2}{(m_{20}+m_{02})^2}$
        \citep{jaehne}, 0 for a round region, 1 for a line.
  \item \textbf{Symmetry.} Boundary-based, after \citet{bollmann1997}: Gabor edge
        energy in 12 orientations summed in pairs of boxes on opposite sides of a
        point, over radii 6--15 at three scales (thesis Tab.~5.1).
  \item \textbf{Depth} from a rectified stereo pair: windowed normalised
        cross-correlation of Gabor magnitudes at a few near-vertical
        orientations over a disparity range, spatially integrated, winner-take-all
        per pixel (eq.~5.13--5.16); and \textbf{onset} for image sequences.
  \item \textbf{Exclusivity.} A feature value shared by $n$ regions is divided
        by $c^{\,n}$, $c = 1.1$: common is less salient than unique.
  \item \textbf{Selection} by a two-dimensional neural field of the Amari type
        \citep{amari1977}: local excitation, broad and global inhibition; the
        clusters of activation that survive are the candidates handed to stage
        two. The field, not a fixed count, decides how many there are.
\end{itemize}
The lineage is the familiar one---parallel feature maps, a fusion into saliency,
a selection dynamics \citep{treisman1980,koch1985,itti1998,borji2013survey}---and
three things differ. The features are computed on \emph{regions} rather than on
centre--surround differences between pixels, so what becomes salient is already
a candidate object. Every feature map is on an absolute scale, not normalised to
its own maximum, so ``nothing here is salient'' is representable; that turns out
to matter more than it sounds (Section~\ref{sec:findingA}). And the selection
step returns \emph{several} surviving clusters rather than one maximum, which is
what makes a second stage possible at all.

\subsection{Stage two: object files and inhibition of return on objects}
Each activity cluster of the field gets an \emph{object file} \citep{kahneman1992}:
a symbolic record holding the region's position, extent and trajectory, its mean
feature values and a leaky-integrated history of them, when it was created, when
it was last selected, and whatever later processing has concluded about it. The
number of files is small by construction---the field sustains only a few
clusters---which is the model's nod to the evidence that human vision indexes
only a handful of objects at a time \citep{pylyshyn1988}. Between frames, files
and clusters are put into correspondence by spatial proximity and feature
similarity (\S7.2.3, the exact rule matters in Section~\ref{sec:chain}); a file
whose cluster disappears becomes inactive but is kept, so that a reappearing
object can reclaim its history rather than start again.

The second selection step chooses one file. It operates on the symbols alone:
the \emph{behaviour} sees positions, saliencies, selection times and labels, not
the image. The default behaviour, ``exploration'', ranks files by how long ago
they were last focally selected---never-selected files first, then the
longest-unselected, ties broken by saliency---and dwells on its choice for about
the duration of one attentive computation. That ranking \emph{is} inhibition of
return \citep{posner1984,klein2000}, applied to an object: because the file
follows its object, the inhibition travels with the object instead of staying
where the object used to be, as the object-centred effects in human vision
suggest it should \citep{tipper1991}. The claim to be tested is that in a
dynamic scene this beats inhibiting locations.

\paragraph{Related work.} Object-based attention in computer vision was being
built at the same time and has continued since. \citet{sun2003}, extended in
\citet{sun2008}, is the closest contemporary in ambition---hierarchical
selection over features, objects and groupings, with eye movements---and the
2008 paper presents itself as the first implemented machine-vision model
integrating object- and space-based attention. The proto-object line runs from
\citet{walther2006} through \citet{orabona2007} to the border-ownership models
of \citet{russell2014} and \citet{uejima2020}; \citet{wischnewski2010} share the
two-stage framing most directly, with a selection stage over static and dynamic
proto-objects. Whether objects predict fixations better than early saliency was
argued out on the same data by \citet{einhauser2008} and \citet{borji2013objects}.
Within the group the 2004 system came from, the region-based line continued with
\citet{aziz2007}. On the field side, \citet{zibner2011} is the nearest prior art
to this architecture---dynamic neural fields building a scene representation by
controlling gaze---with the difference that there the persisting peak \emph{is}
the object; there is no symbolic layer above it. Where identity has to survive
more than a peak can carry, that work adds an explicit association step outside
the field \citep{kamkar2022}, and recent field-based robotic search again needs
two kinds of inhibition of return \citep{grieben2024}. The modern descendant of
the object file is the persistent slot of object-centric learning
\citep{locatello2020,peters2021}.

\paragraph{The claim itself has been tested once, in 2023.} \citet{roth2023}
build five scanpath models over dynamic real-world video and compare them
against human gaze, among them a mixed model that is a single-module ablation:
object-based attention and selection, but \emph{space}-based inhibition of
return. Their overall finding favours object-based attention. On the specific
question they are more careful than this paper's claim would like: they report
that object-based \emph{selection} influences return-related measures more than
the inhibition mechanism does, and they decline to decide whether inhibition of
return is bound to objects or to locations, because their data contain too few
return saccades. \citet{mengers2025} then reproduce the delay of returning
saccades with no explicit inhibition of return at all, from segmentation
uncertainty. The psychophysics the 2004 model relied on is likewise contested:
\citet{tipper1991} found object-centred inhibition, \citet{mueller1996} found
none in moving displays, and \citet{krueger2013} make it depend on prediction.
Two implications for what follows. First, the comparison this paper runs---with
latency to new objects and degradation with object speed as the dependent
measures---has not been made before, in 2004 or since. Second, an intermediate
position exists that the 2004 model does not consider: inhibition anchored in
\emph{world} rather than image coordinates, as in the ego-sphere of
\citet{ruesch2008}, which survives camera motion without knowing about objects.
Section~\ref{sec:realvideo} effectively runs that baseline.

\section{Reimplementation and protocol}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{The reimplementation}
C++17 and OpenCV, with a Python evaluation layer; every component is selected by
a configuration file, and every model emits one interchange format, which is all
the evaluation code reads. The replication is defined as a set of configuration
profiles (\texttt{configs/thesis/}) that may enable dissertation components only
(enforced by a test), plus fidelity tests and behavioural goldens. It is frozen
at the tag \texttt{replication-v1}; later work on a modernised system adds
sibling components and does not edit these. \src{docs/adr/0005}
\paragraph{How the code was written.} The reimplementation and the evaluation
code were written by the author together with an AI coding assistant, which
wrote most of the code and much of the measurement infrastructure. What that
does and does not mean for this paper: the author read and checked by hand every
port against the original sources, every parameter in Appendix~\ref{app:params},
every verdict in the dossier against the thesis's own figures, and every
prediction before it was committed. All three infidelities this paper reports
arose in that process---a relative normalisation where the original thresholded
absolutely, parameters taken from a function's default arguments, and a
convergence test where the deployed system ran a fixed number of cycles---and
none of them was caught by reading the code. All three were caught by the
replication protocol, which is part of the argument of
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} and is the reason that argument is worth making
whoever, or whatever, writes the code. \todo{adjust to the
venue's disclosure policy.}

\subsection{Sources of truth, and what cannot be shown}
\label{sec:sources}
Three sources, in order of authority for this paper:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The thesis text} (open access, in German) is what a reader can
        check, so the documented replication profile follows the text wherever
        text and code disagree.
  \item \textbf{The surviving original sources} (2003--2005, C++) cover the
        static features, the stereo feature, the neural fields and the program
        that configured the dissertation system. They settled what the text
        leaves open---most importantly the field's parameters, which the thesis
        describes only qualitatively. They cannot be published. Appendix~\ref{app:params}
        lists every value taken from them, next to the text's.
  \item \textbf{The thesis's figures}, as behavioural targets: the replication
        bar is loose behavioural equivalence, not pixel equality. The thesis's
        laboratory images did not survive, except the running example of
        Abb.~5.8--5.22; stimuli were rebuilt from the figures' descriptions.
\end{enumerate}
Where the two disagree, a second profile carrying the deployed system's values
is kept runnable, and Section~\ref{sec:textcode} compares them.

\subsection{Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}
The rules below were adopted after an internal review found that earlier results
in this project had been tuned and tested on the same few seeds.
\src{docs/CRITICAL\_REVIEW\_2026-09.md, docs/HYPOTHESIS\_CLOSURE\_PLAN.md}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Development and test data are disjoint.} Scenes with seeds 0--9
        shape the instrument; confirmatory runs use fresh blocks (1000--1029,
        2000--2029, 3000--3029), generated only after the predictions are
        committed.
  \item \textbf{Predictions are committed to the repository before the run},
        with a refutation criterion each (H1: commit \texttt{ab7d75e}; human
        gaze: \texttt{36761d8}). This is a public, timestamped record, not a
        registration with a third party.
  \item \textbf{The arm expected to lose gets a strengthened version}, and every
        comparison a trivial baseline.
  \item \textbf{Paired bootstrap} over independent units (scenes, images), 4000
        resamples, 95\% intervals; a difference ``holds'' if its interval
        excludes zero.
  \item \textbf{Verdicts} for the dossier: \emph{replicated} (holds, measured),
        \emph{partially}, \emph{diverged} (with the reason), \emph{not attempted}
        (with the reason).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Features}
Where the thesis gives an equation, the reimplementation can be held to it.
\textbf{Eccentricity} is defined to be 0 for a round region and 1 for a line, and
is: 0.000 for a disk and for a square, 0.354 for an ellipse of axis ratio 2:1
where eq.~5.6 gives 0.360, 0.934 for a bar of 8:1 where it gives 0.940. Stretching
one object of constant area from 1:1 to 6:1 (Figure~\ref{fig:ecc}) tracks eq.~5.6
to within 0.02 at every step, while its \textbf{symmetry} response falls
monotonically, $0.67 \to 0.15$: the two features trade places as the thesis's
figure shows. \textbf{Colour contrast} rises monotonically as a blob's colour
moves away from its ground, $0.00 \to 0.32 \to 0.81 \to 0.98 \to 1.00$, saturating
after a third of the way---the sigmoid of eq.~5.12 is steep on a contrast scale
of 32 Munsell units, which the thesis's own argument (``high values are rare
overall'') intends. Its two thresholds are insensitive over the range the thesis
claims and beyond it, and the same two regions of the thesis's running example
(a ball and a picture on a wall) stay the salient ones throughout; the variance
term in the contrast earns its place, since without it the ball's response drops
from 0.71 to 0.12. \textbf{Exclusivity} gives the odd one out the gain the
formula predicts: five vertical bars and one horizontal, ratio odd-to-common
$1.03 \to 1.51$ where $1.1^4 = 1.46$; for colour the following sigmoid stretches
it further, to 2.13. Under added noise (20 seeds per level) the eccentricity
maximum stays on its bar for 19 of 20 seeds at $\sigma \approx 38$ grey levels,
14 of 20 at 64, and is lost beyond; the colour and symmetry maxima stay on their
objects for every seed at every level tested, up to $\sigma \approx 115$.
\src{dossier, findings 1--9}

\paragraph{A trap for synthetic stimuli.} The eccentricity feature
histogram-equalises its input, as the original does. On an image with three grey
levels that maps a \emph{bright} object and a dominant background to nearly the
same grey, the merge criterion then swallows the object, and the feature
reports nothing where a human sees a bar. It does not arise on natural images,
whose histograms are full; it arises exactly on the kind of clean synthetic
stimulus a replication builds, and the same caution applies to the scenes of
Section~\ref{sec:h1}.

\paragraph{The two partial verdicts.} The growth threshold (Abb.~5.15) is stable
in the range the thesis names, but the map correlation with the default is only
0.64--0.78 there; ``plausible'' in the thesis is a visual judgement and a
pixel-wise correlation is a stricter reading. Identity through occlusion
(Abb.~6.14) is tracked in the object files here, not in the fields, so the figure
is not reproduced as such; the question is measured in Section~\ref{sec:h1}
(occlusion regime, Table~\ref{tab:h1}: 2.8 labels per attended object with
position-only correspondence, 1.5 with feature-led revival of inactive object
files). The verdict was re-examined once the correspondence rule of \S7.2.3 was
implemented as written, since the first reading had attributed the failure to
the thesis when what had been measured was position-only matching: on a fresh
block it gives 2.7 labels per attended object against 2.8 for position only and
1.4 for an appearance descriptor with a memory that does not expire. The verdict
stands, with the attribution corrected---the rule the thesis specifies does not
hold identity through a ten-frame occlusion on these scenes, and what does is not
in the 2004 model.

\subsection{Depth}
The depth feature is exercised on rendered random-dot pairs with known
disparity, which carry no monocular cue---so every stimulus is also the
random-dot stereogram of the thesis's Abb.~5.29. For a surface shifted by
$0, 2, \ldots, 14$~px the estimated disparity is $0.0, 2.0, \ldots, 14.0$, with
every surface pixel within one pixel of the truth and the ground at zero; the
stereogram's square, whose mean grey differs from its ground by 3 of 255 and
which is invisible in either image alone, is recovered completely. Under
independent noise in the two images the estimate for a surface at 10~px is 10.0
up to $\sigma \approx 38$, 9.99 at 64 and 9.91 at 90---``the estimate changes
only in a very small range'', as the thesis says. The thesis credits its
multi-scale scheme for that robustness; the scheme is \emph{not} ported, and the
single-scale correlation with the spatial integration of eq.~5.14 is already
this robust. The finding replicates; the thesis's explanation of it is untested
here. Several near-vertical orientations help and the exact choice beyond one
matters little (correct surface pixels at $\sigma = 90$: 0.84 for vertical only,
0.92 with $\pm30^\circ$, 0.91 with $\pm15^\circ$ and $\pm30^\circ$), as claimed.

The variance threshold, which rejects windows too flat to be matched, behaves as
the thesis describes on a synthetic scene: below about 5 a textureless band is
admitted and 89\% of its pixels get a wrong disparity; from about 20 the band is
rejected; from 40 upward correct pixels start to go, the faint-textured surface
first. But the practical advice that follows is the opposite of what that
experiment alone suggests, and the first version of this dossier got it wrong.
On three Middlebury pairs with ground truth \citep{scharstein2002}---data the
thesis never saw---raising the threshold never buys fewer wrong pixels (their
share stays at 12--20\% throughout) and only discards correct ones. The reason
is visible when pixels are split by local texture: on the 40--50\% of each image
with little grey-value variation, 81--86\% of the disparities are correct at
threshold zero, because the spatial integration carries them from their textured
surroundings. The errors sit at occlusions and depth discontinuities, which no
variance threshold addresses. At the port's default the feature gets 82--85\% of
all pixels with ground truth right, which is an independent check of the
thesis's own summary of this feature (``in the great majority of regions the
disparity has been determined correctly'').

\subsection{Finding A: a symmetry feature that answered beside its objects}
\label{sec:findingA}
For a single bright disk on a grey ground, the first reimplementation's symmetry
map responded inside the disk \emph{and equally strongly about 80~px to either
side of it}; the global maximum lay off the object for every disk size tried,
without noise. The summation core was a faithful port. What differed was what
followed it: the original works on an absolute scale---edge magnitudes clipped,
then a fixed offset (60 of 255) subtracted from every radius band---which removes
the response that an edge on \emph{one} side alone produces. The summation is
additive over the two opposite boxes, so a lone edge scores a large share of a
closed contour's response---measured against a disk of the same contrast, 0.78,
0.40 and 0.32 for radius 12, 24 and 40~px. The share is not constant: it depends
on how the object's size matches the radius bands, which is exactly why a
relative threshold cannot remove those responses and a fixed offset on an
absolute scale can (with the thesis's 60/255 the lone edge falls to 0.09 of the
disk). The reimplementation had instead normalised each band to its
own maximum and applied relative thresholds. With no absolute reference, a
one-sided response is as strong as a true centre and appears as a ring around
every object. After porting the combination step, a lone disk peaks at its centre
(0.43 / 0.82 / 0.99 for radius 12 / 24 / 40) and a single straight edge gives
0.08. One thing the sources do not settle is the gain of the original Gabor
implementation, which fixes what ``60 of 255'' means; the calibration chosen (a
full-contrast step edge has energy 1) is a reconstruction.
\src{dossier, finding A; symmetry\_feature.h}

\subsection{Findings C--E: what a second source exposed}
\label{sec:findingsCDE}
The dossier was built from the thesis's chapters 5 and 6. Reading the last
English-language paper from the thesis work \citep{backer2003} against the thesis
and the code exposed three more gaps: (C) the thesis's own quantitative test of
its central claim had been overlooked (Section~\ref{sec:worldmodel}); (D) the
correspondence the reimplementation called ``the thesis's'' was position-only,
weaker than \S7.2.3; (E) object files were formed from saliency segments, not
from the field's activity clusters. All three were closed
(Sections~\ref{sec:worldmodel} and~\ref{sec:chain}). The surviving original
sources do not contain the second stage, so for it the thesis text and that paper
are the only record. The general lesson is in
Section~\ref{sec:discussion}: a second description of the same system, written
by the same authors for a different audience, is a cheap and unusually effective
fidelity check, because it says different things in different words about the
parts a single document leaves implicit.

\subsection{Text or code?}
\label{sec:textcode}
Where the thesis text and the deployed system disagree, which is ``the model''?
They disagree in the eccentricity thresholds, the colour thresholds and the
sigmoid's slope, in whether exclusivity is on at all, and in the feature weights
(equal in the text, $1.2 / 0.35 / 1.6$ for symmetry, eccentricity and colour in
the system). Both are kept runnable and compared on every benchmark the
repository can run without a language model, paired on identical items. Neither
is decisively better. The text's values are the closer fit to the thesis's own
equations---the system adds a saliency offset that takes eccentricity off
eq.~5.6, a 2:1 ellipse scoring 0.19 instead of 0.35---and are slightly ahead
where a difference is measurable at all: on MIT1003 the model's own scanpath
scores $0.016$ [$0.008$, $0.023$] ScanMatch higher with the text's values. On
target coverage and on visual search every interval includes zero. The
object-file readout's position agreement is the one place the system's values
lead, by $0.008$ [$0.001$, $0.015$]. On the thesis's running example the two
colour maps correlate at 0.92 and the two eccentricity maps at 0.59: eccentricity
is where the variants really differ, and it is also the feature the system
down-weights most, which is probably why so little of the difference reaches a
benchmark.

The documented replication profile follows the \emph{text}, because the text is
what a reader can check; the system's values are a sibling profile. The general
point is worth stating for other replications: when the text and the surviving
code of an old system disagree, report both, say which one the reader can
verify, and check that the paper's conclusions do not depend on the choice.
Here they do not---the central claim holds under either parameter set
(Section~\ref{sec:h1}).

\section{Study 2: object-based versus space-based inhibition of return}
\label{sec:h1}

\paragraph{The claim (H1).} The thesis states the mechanism's purpose in its
introduction (\S1; all translations mine): ``tracking a small number of elements
\ldots allows object-based processing in dynamic scenes to be improved and,
among other things, an object-based inhibition of recently selected objects to be
achieved''. Its evidence is the experiment of \S9.2, whose result it states as:
``the classical system profits from the faster object recognition in scenes
without dynamic objects, but as soon as dynamic objects are present its
performance drops against the presented model, which scales well with the
additional number of dynamic objects''---with the position error of the
two-stage model always below 0.5~px and that of the classical model between 0.5
and 5~px. Operationally, for this study: in multi-object dynamic scenes,
object-based inhibition of return yields lower novel-object latency than
space-based inhibition and degrades gracefully with object speed. (References to
the thesis are by section, not page: it is open access and its sectioning is
unambiguous, whereas its pagination differs between the deposited versions.)

\subsection{The thesis's own test}
\label{sec:worldmodel}
The thesis contains one quantitative test of its central claim (\S9.2; the main
result of \citealp{backer2003}), which the first version of the dossier, built
from chapters 5--6, had missed. Two models explore the same simulated master map
of attention and each keeps a world model---which object is where. Objects are
$5\times5$ pixels, static or moving on straight paths by at most 2~px per axis
and frame, at least 14~px apart, under uniform noise of half their amplitude;
1, 3 or 5 static and 0--5 dynamic objects; 50 runs of 40 frames per condition.
Recognition is simulated: it names the object under the focus after 3 frames
(conventional) or 4 (two-stage).
The \emph{conventional} model blurs, subtracts a decaying inhibition map ($8\times8$
marks, $\times0.8$ per frame) and takes the maximum; an identity stays bound to the
place where its object was selected. The \emph{two-stage} model is the thesis's
simplest variant---one 2D field of local inhibition, object files on its activity
clusters with the correspondence of \S7.2.3, the exploration behaviour---and an
identity moves with its object file. Measures: the mean number of objects whose
identity the world model holds within 20~px of the truth, and the mean position
error of those. What the sources leave open (map size, velocity distribution,
noise mean, when the conventional model's recognition looks at the focus) was
fixed on development seeds, the last in the conventional model's favour; two
variants were declared in advance; predictions were committed before the 50
confirmatory runs (\texttt{3edd62f}). The run was repeated once after the
cross-platform check found that its scenes depended on the C++ standard library
(Section~\ref{sec:repro}); the numbers below are the repeat, and the two runs
agree except where noted. \src{dossier, finding 22; eval/replication.py --only world-model}

\paragraph{Result (Figure~\ref{fig:worldmodel}).} The two-stage model is within
0.01 of its optimum---every object recognized at the first opportunity and never
lost---in all 18 conditions, and its curve is the thesis's curve (within 0.25 in
17 of 18 conditions). The field produces exactly as many activity clusters as
there are objects in every condition. Its position error is 0.53--0.95~px; the
conventional model's grows with the number of moving objects to 4--7~px. Pooled
over the static counts, two-stage minus conventional recognized objects for 0--5
dynamic objects: $-0.18$, $-0.19$, $-0.10$, $+0.03$, $+0.30$, $+0.41$, every
interval but the one at three moving objects excluding zero. So the thesis's
scaling claim and its position-error claim replicate in direction and size; two
statements do not: ``ahead as soon as anything moves'' (here: from four moving
objects on, and from three on the first sample of scenes---that step is the size
of the scene-to-scene variation) and ``below 0.5~px in every condition''. The first is the baseline's doing: the conventional model rebuilt
from the description reaches its own optimum in static scenes (4.00 with five
objects) where the thesis's reached about 3.3, so the two-stage model's extra
frame per recognition is paid back later. Why the thesis's baseline fell short
the sources do not say. All five predictions held in their stated refutation
criteria; two were imprecise in a detail (the crossover sits one step later than
guessed, and positive-mean noise costs the lead at three moving objects rather
than at four and five).

\paragraph{A third parameter finding.} Building this experiment exposed what
Finding~B had missed: over a stream the deployed system ran its field for a
\emph{fixed 20 cycles per frame} (its convergence test compared a summed change
with a threshold no noisy field meets) with an input gain of 0.765. With the
port's convergence test the field stops after about three cycles, a moving
object's cluster stays behind, self-sustained, and the object file stays with the
ghost.

This experiment measures what the system \emph{knows}; the study below measures
where attention \emph{goes}.

\paragraph{Metrics.} Primary: \emph{latency}, the frames from an object's

\subsection{Three runs, and why the third one stands}
\label{sec:threeruns}
The result above is the third answer this study gave to the same question. The
first two were wrong, and they were wrong in a way worth reporting, because
nothing about them looked careless at the time.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Exploration} (July--September; $\le6$ seeds, no intervals):
        space-based inhibition at least as good as object-based, ``usually
        better''. Explained at the time, plausibly, by tracking errors: object-based
        inhibition is only as good as its tracker. Recorded in the project as an
        honest negative result.
  \item \textbf{First confirmatory run} (seeds 1000--1029), after the colour and
        eccentricity features had been ported from the original: object-based
        inhibition wins on latency in all regimes, but not on staleness; a third
        of its fixations land on no object. Explained at the time, again
        plausibly, by segmentation: object files that are not objects.
  \item \textbf{Re-run} (seeds 2000--2029), after Study~1 had found and fixed the
        symmetry feature (Finding~A)---a change decided by a test that does not
        involve inhibition of return. Same command, same arms, same committed
        predictions.
\end{enumerate}

Each explanation was mechanistically sensible and each was tested further. The
tracker explanation predicted that a better tracker would close the gap; motion
prediction and an appearance descriptor were added, they cut object-based
inhibition's wasted revisits threefold, and the gap narrowed but did not close,
which was read as confirmation that the bottleneck was real but deeper. The
segmentation explanation was supported by a directly measured quantity: a third
of the object-based arms' fixations landed on no object at all, so object files
that were not objects were demonstrably being attended. Both explanations
survived the obvious follow-up test. Neither was true of the 2004 model. The
off-object third was the symmetry feature's rings---salient clusters in the empty
space beside every disk, which only an object-based arm would select, because a
location tag left on a moving disk frees the disk again and the disk outranks a
ring. When the feature was ported faithfully, that share went to zero in every
arm and the comparison changed sign.

What makes the third run the one that stands is not that its answer is the one
the thesis wanted. It is that the change between the second and the third was
decided \emph{elsewhere}: by a single-disk experiment for a feature, judged
against the thesis's Abb.~5.13 and 5.14, with no reference to inhibition of
return; that the predictions were the second run's, committed before the first
and left unchanged; and that they were pessimistic---three of the four were
refuted, all in the direction of the thesis being right. A result that arrives by
refuting one's own predictions is worth more than one that arrives by confirming
them.

\subsection{The thesis's own chain, and its own correspondence}
\label{sec:chain}
Two things separate the runs above from the dissertation system
(Section~\ref{sec:findingsCDE}): correspondence there was position-only, where
thesis \S7.2.3 also compares feature means and revives inactive object files by
their features; and the object files were formed from segments of the saliency
map, where the thesis forms them from the neural field's activity clusters. Both
were implemented as written and measured on a fourth fresh block (seeds
4000--4029), predictions committed first (\texttt{5325d2e}).

\emph{Correspondence.} On saliency segments the rule of \S7.2.3 changes little:
no difference to position-only correspondence in latency in any regime; at high
speed fewer labels per object (4.2 against 5.8) and slightly better staleness
($-0.16$ [$-0.31$, $-0.05$]). Every object-based arm again beats both spatial arms
on latency and staleness in every regime.

\emph{The chain.} With the field at the dissertation system's size (64~px) the
first stage holds about three of the four disks at a time, and the whole chain
loses to the segment-based spatial reference on staleness in every regime,
whatever the behaviour ($+1.6$ to $+1.9$ frames at low speed). At twice that
size all objects fit, and the result splits cleanly along the field's tracking
range (Table~\ref{tab:chain}): at 2.4 field pixels per frame object-based
inhibition on the thesis's chain beats both spatial behaviours on the same
clusters, and is the best system of the whole block (latency 1.62, staleness
1.68); at 8 and 16 field pixels per frame the field hands the second stage a new
object file every few frames (nine to ten labels per object) and object-based
inhibition becomes the \emph{worst} behaviour.

The replication claim rests on the chain, because the chain is the dissertation
system; the segment-based runs are reported as what the thesis's proposed
extension does. So the claim holds \emph{wherever the first stage delivers objects that can be
tracked}: with a segment-based first stage at every speed tested, with the
thesis's neural field inside the tracking range the thesis itself states (``an
object movement of more than 12 pixels''). What the thesis does not say is that
beyond that range object-based inhibition is not merely no better than the
location tag it set out to replace, but worse. The high-speed result of
Table~\ref{tab:h1} is a result about the segment-based variant---which
\citet{backer2003} propose in their last sentence as an extension---not about
the dissertation system. A fourth regime, occlusion at trackable speed (the situation of thesis
Abb.~6.14; predicted and run afterwards, \texttt{8045e29}), confirms it:
object-based inhibition on the chain keeps its advantage through a 10-frame
occlusion ($-1.09$ [$-1.95$, $-0.42$] latency, $-0.73$ [$-1.09$, $-0.46$]
staleness against space-based on the same clusters) and is again the best arm
of the block. All four predictions held, except a detail of the
second (inside the 64-px chain object-based inhibition \emph{was} better than
space-based at low speed and under occlusion).

\paragraph{What the claim's wording does not survive.} ``Higher object
coverage'': no difference; every arm with inhibition sees every object within 40
frames. Space-based inhibition ``collapses'' with speed: it degrades badly
(latency 2.61~$\to$~6.08) but still sees every object. Identity matters less than
the exploration phase concluded: persistent identity brings labels per object
from 5.5 to 1.7 at high speed and adds a further 0.4 frames; position-only
correspondence already carries most of the advantage.

\subsection{Real video}
\label{sec:realvideo}
Every number so far is from synthetic scenes. DAVIS~2017 \citep{ponttuset2017}
provides natural video with per-frame instance masks, which serve as ground
truth for ``which object was attended'' (a focus on an object's mask, dilated
by 12~px). Test set: the 17 validation sequences with at least two annotated
objects (34--99 frames, 2--5 objects); development on eight training sequences;
all arms of Table~\ref{tab:arms} on both first stages; predictions committed
before any test sequence was scored (\texttt{ba3ab2d}). Most sequences pan, so
every profile was also run with the second stage's coordinates moved by a
phase-correlation estimate of the camera's shift (thesis \S7.2.2 adjusts
coordinates by the camera movement, known there from the platform).
\src{docs/DYNAMIC\_IOR\_STUDY.md, ``Real video''}

\paragraph{Result.} The evaluation cannot decide the claim, and the reason is
visible before any comparison: between 42\% and 61\% of every arm's fixations
land on no annotated object---on the crowd behind the dancers, the tents behind
the tractor, the trees. They are salient; DAVIS annotates what a segmentation
benchmark cares about, not what a bottom-up system should attend. On the
segment-based first stage no difference between object-based and space-based
inhibition excludes zero (staleness $-0.6$ to $-0.9$ frames in the object-based
arms' favour, 95\% intervals including zero); on the thesis's chain the one
difference that excludes zero goes against it (latency $+1.53$ [$+0.22$,
$+3.39$] against the motion-compensated tag), consistent with the synthetic
finding that object-based inhibition on the field is only as good as the
field's clusters, which on natural video are few and large. Camera compensation
did not change the ordering and, where it changed a number with an interval
excluding zero, made it worse. One unpredicted observation: object-based
inhibition on segments puts a sixth more of its fixations on the annotated
objects than space-based inhibition does (42--45\% against 60\%)---an inhibited
object file stays inhibited as it moves; what a location tag frees is the
object, what an object tag frees is the background.

This is a limitation of the test, and it is stated as one: the claim concerns
inhibition among \emph{attended} objects, and a test on natural video needs a
first stage or a ground truth that agree on what the objects are. It is not
evidence for the claim on real video. The tests that carry H1 remain
Sections~\ref{sec:worldmodel} and~\ref{sec:chain}.

\section{Study 3: scanpaths against human gaze}
\label{sec:h4}

\paragraph{The question (H4).} Do the model's scanpaths on still images lie
above random and central baselines toward the agreement between human observers,
and does the second stage's ordering help? The thesis does not claim to model
free viewing; this study asks how far its mechanism is from doing so.

\subsection{Method}
MIT1003 \citep{judd2009}: all 1003 images, 15 observers, per-observer fixation
sequences recovered from the raw eye-tracking samples with a dispersion-threshold
filter. ScanMatch \citep{cristino2010} as primary score, the four spatial
dimensions of MultiMatch \citep{dewhurst2012} alongside; both implemented in the
repository and unit-tested. Arms: leave-one-out inter-observer agreement
(ceiling); the model's own fixations (\arm{thesis-field}); the same saliency map
read out by the second stage (\arm{thesis-objfile}) and by a generic
winner-take-all with inhibition (\arm{thesis-wta}); a stochastic readout; spectral
residual \citep{hou2007}; a central prior; a constant central path; random. No
learned model was run, by decision: this study's arms are the 2004 model and
the baselines it should be judged against, and adding a modern learned
predictor would compare two things the paper is not comparing. It is
nevertheless a gap---DeepGaze \citep{kummerer2016} is the obvious upper
reference point between the model and the inter-observer ceiling, and without it
the floor-to-ceiling axis has no modern marker on it. Predictions
committed before the run (\texttt{36761d8}). \src{docs/SCANPATH\_VS\_HUMAN.md}

\subsection{Two findings about the instrument}
\label{sec:instrument}

\paragraph{What a constant path is worth.} On MIT1003 the constant central path
scores 0.754 ScanMatch against an inter-observer ceiling of 0.759. That looked
like a statement about centre bias, and centre bias is real---viewing starts in
the middle and MIT1003 is strongly centre-biased \citep{tatler2007}. But
\citet{schwinn2022} report the opposite on the same dataset under a string-edit
scanpath score: their centre baseline ``did not perform much better than
Random''. The difference between the two baselines is not centrality. Theirs
draws each fixation independently from a central Gaussian; ours returns the same
path every time. Running both on the same 200 images settles it
(Table~\ref{tab:centre}): the constant path sits within 0.01 of the ceiling,
while the sampled one is 0.07 below it and only 0.05 above random. The gain is
\emph{determinism}, not location---a repeatable path is rewarded, whatever it
repeats.

This is \citet{kummerer2021}'s argument---that under these metrics a wrong but
deterministic model can outscore the process that generated the data---appearing
on real data and at the size that matters for a benchmark. The practical
consequence for anyone scoring scanpaths on a centre-biased dataset is that a
trivial constant path is the baseline to beat, and that a stochastic baseline
will understate how easy the metric is to satisfy. MultiMatch's direction
dimension is the one measure here that separates the constant path from the
observers (0.501 against a ceiling of 0.630, the lowest of all arms).

\paragraph{Path length.} That a string-edit measure confounds similarity with
length is known: \citet{jarodzka2010} state it plainly---``a long scanpath is per
default dissimilar to a short one, even though the shorter is a substring of the
longer''---and \citet{mathot2012} call normalisation for sequence length
``inherently problematic''. The mechanism in ScanMatch is the normalisation by
the \emph{longer} of the two sequences, which caps a $k$-fixation path scored
against an $m$-fixation one at about $k/m$ however well its $k$ fixations match;
the gap value is a second-order effect (Table~\ref{tab:centre} shows the
conclusions are the same at both). What this study adds is the case where it
bites hardest and is least expected: a model that chooses its \emph{own} number
of fixations. With the deployed system's field parameters the model made four
fixations instead of ten, and its unmatched ScanMatch fell \emph{below random}
(0.544 against 0.636) while its position, shape and length agreement all
\emph{rose} (+0.037, +0.019, +0.055). A model can thus be made to look worse
than chance by giving it better parameters. Everyone in this literature seems to
match length silently---by truncating to a fixed number of fixations---and
nobody states it as a requirement; on this evidence it should be one whenever
the compared paths are not the same length by construction.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{A negative result about a reimplementation}
For most of this study's duration its written record said that the thesis's
central claim fails. That record was not sloppy. It reported what had been
measured, it named its own weaknesses, it offered a mechanism, and when the
mechanism made a prediction the prediction was tested. It was still an artefact
of two lines of code.

The useful question is what would have caught it sooner, and the answer is
uncomfortable, because the instrument that caught it was available from the
start and is smaller than anything else in this paper: a white disk on a grey
ground, with the question ``does the symmetry feature peak on it?''. That is the
thesis's own Abb.~5.13, one of the demonstrations a modern reader is inclined to
skip past on the way to the results. It fails in a single glance for the
defective feature---the map's maximum lies about 80~px beside the disk, without
noise, at every disk size---and it is the only test in this study that does.
Every downstream measurement was corrupted by the same defect, and not one of
them pointed at it: the object-coverage benchmarks reported plausible numbers,
the scanpath study reported plausible numbers, and the H1 comparison reported a
clean, interpretable, wrong result with an explanation attached.

The same holds for the second infidelity. The neural field's parameters had been
taken from the default arguments of the original's setter functions rather than
from the program that configured the deployed system---eight times too little
global inhibition, among other differences. Under those values the field's
clusters never merge, noise leaks in at a third of the amplitude the thesis
reports, and moving targets are lost; every one of those is visible in the
thesis's own Abb.~6.4--6.10, and in nothing else that was being measured. A
third instance appeared later still: over a stream the deployed system ran its
field for a fixed number of cycles per frame, because its convergence test could
never be met on noisy input, and the port's faithful-looking convergence check
stopped it after three cycles and left ghost clusters behind moving objects
(Section~\ref{sec:worldmodel}).

Three infidelities, three different ways of being wrong about the same
system---an algorithmic step, a parameter's provenance, a control-flow detail
that no equation mentions---and in all three cases the unit-level experiment of
the original found it and the headline benchmark did not. That is the case for
replicating an old model's smallest demonstrations first, even when they look
like decoration.

Lessons, as far as one case supports them:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Replicate the original's unit-level experiments before its
        headline claim.} The downstream benchmark was corrupted by both
        infidelities and pointed at neither.
  \item \textbf{Absolute versus relative scales are not an implementation
        detail.} Min--max normalisation, the default idiom of saliency code since
        \citet{itti1998}, erased exactly the information (``this response is too
        weak to be a symmetry'') that the original's fixed offset encoded.
  \item \textbf{Parameter provenance: defaults are not the deployed
        configuration.} The values in a function signature and the values the
        system ran with differed by a factor of eight in the term that decides
        whether two clusters merge.
  \item \textbf{Pessimistic predictions are still predictions.} Three of four
        failed. Committing them beforehand is what makes ``the claim holds''
        more than a preference---particularly for an author testing his own work.
  \item \textbf{A plausible mechanism for a negative result is not evidence for
        it.}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{What the thesis got right, and what it overstated}
\textbf{Right.} The features behave as described, quantitatively where an
equation is given. The neural field behaves as described---hysteresis, merging,
noise suppression, tracking limits---once it runs on the parameters the system
actually used. The depth feature recovers disparity exactly on synthetic pairs
and correctly on four fifths of real ones. The world model the two-stage
architecture maintains is the one the thesis reports, to the second decimal.
And the central claim holds: with a first stage that delivers trackable objects,
inhibition of return on objects reaches new objects sooner and keeps a scene more
evenly covered than inhibition of return on locations, against a plain and a
motion-compensated location tag, on four independent blocks of scenes.

\textbf{Overstated, or true for a reason the thesis does not give.} ``Higher
object coverage'' does not hold: every arm with any inhibition sees every object
within 40 frames, and coverage is simply the wrong quantity. Space-based
inhibition ``collapses'' with speed only in the sense of degrading badly; it
still finds everything. The thesis's own comparison ``as soon as objects move''
is earlier than this study can confirm: a conventional model built from the
thesis's description is stronger than the thesis's own baseline was, and the
two-stage model's extra cost per recognition is repaid only from three moving
objects on. The robustness of the depth feature under noise is real but is not
explained by the multi-scale scheme the thesis credits, since a single scale
already has it. And identity through occlusion, which the thesis treats as a
property of the architecture, is not delivered by the correspondence rule the
thesis specifies---it needs an appearance descriptor and a memory that does not
expire, neither of which is in the 2004 model.

\textbf{Not claimed, and not found.} The model is not a model of human free
viewing. Its scanpaths are slightly better than chance and well short of a
constant central path on MIT1003 (Section~\ref{sec:h4}). The thesis never says
otherwise; it is worth stating because a reader in 2026 will reach for that
comparison first.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Self-replication.} Author, reimplementer and evaluator are one
        person. The protocol constrains this; it does not remove it.
  \item \textbf{The original sources cannot be released.} Statements of the form
        ``ported from the original'' are verifiable against the thesis text and
        by behaviour (Study~1), not against the code. Appendix~\ref{app:params}
        reports every value taken from it.
  \item \textbf{Rebuilt stimuli.} Except for one running example the thesis's
        images did not survive; experiments rebuild the kind of stimulus a figure
        describes.
  \item \textbf{H1 rests on synthetic scenes}; the natural-video test
        (Section~\ref{sec:realvideo}) is inconclusive for a stated reason. One
        scene generator, disks of distinct colours.
  \item \textbf{Scope.} Two partial verdicts; not attempted: superposition and
        2D-versus-3D integration (Abb.~5.33, 5.35), systems of several fields
        (6.11, 6.12; not implemented), the 3D field's dynamics (6.13), the
        qualitative demonstrations of thesis \S9.3.
  \item \textbf{Two first stages.} The main H1 result (Table~\ref{tab:h1}) is
        measured with object files on saliency segments; on the thesis's own
        chain the claim holds only inside the field's tracking range and with a
        field large enough for the scene (Section~\ref{sec:chain}). The fused
        map that feeds the field is min--max normalised per frame, unlike the
        original's clipped sum.
  \item \textbf{The model changed between the two confirmatory H1 runs}
        (Section~\ref{sec:threeruns}); both are reported.
  \item \textbf{No learned saliency model} in Study~3.
  \item \textbf{No head-to-head with the one prior test of the claim.}
        \citet{roth2023} ask a version of the same question on human gaze over
        natural video, with a different protocol (models fitted to human saccade
        statistics, scored by scanpath similarity). Their setup is not re-run
        here, and this paper's measures---latency to a new object, staleness of
        the world model---are not theirs.
  \item The thesis is in German; quotations are my translations.
\end{itemize}

Continuous integration builds and runs the full test suite---fidelity tests for
every ported feature, behavioural goldens for the thesis profiles---on Linux at
every commit, and Study~1 was run there once in full and compared with the
macOS run value by value: of 1420 compared numbers none differs by more than
$10^{-3}$ and exactly one by more than $10^{-4}$. No verdict depends on the
platform.

That comparison did find one real defect, worth reporting because it is a trap
for any replication of an old experiment. The world-model experiment's scenes
are drawn from a seeded generator, but through
\texttt{std::uniform\_real\_distribution}, which the C++ standard leaves
implementation-defined: the two standard libraries draw different sequences from
the same seed, so the same ``seed 1000'' was a different scene on the two
machines. A seed is not a guarantee of the same data unless the sampler is
pinned as well. The scenes are now drawn by a portable routine, and the re-run
is what Section~\ref{sec:worldmodel} reports; it moves the crossover in that
experiment by one condition and changes nothing else.

With the scenes pinned, a second difference remains, and it is worth a sentence
because it is the thesis's own argument showing up as a reproducibility
property. On identical scenes the two-stage model's scores agree between the two
platforms to four decimals in all 18 conditions, and so does its number of
activity clusters; the conventional model's differ by up to 0.35 recognized
objects. The conventional model selects the maximum of a blurred, noisy map, so
a difference of one unit in the last place can change which pixel wins and the
run diverges from there. A neural field with hysteresis, integrating over space
and time, does not flip on a marginal difference. The verdict of that experiment
is the same on both platforms.
